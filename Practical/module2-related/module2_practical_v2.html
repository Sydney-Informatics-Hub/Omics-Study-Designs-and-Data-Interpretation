What a Count Actually Is

A sequencing count is a proportion of a fixed budget — not an absolute measurement. Explore how sequencing depth shapes what you observe, independently of biology.

Core idea: Gene X received 200 counts in Sample B and 100 in Sample A. Does that mean it's more active? Only if both samples had the same total reads. If Sample B had twice the depth, nothing changed biologically.

Q1Run Apparent FC vs True FC. The right panel shows the apparent fold change (red) next to the true fold change (green). With Sample B at twice the depth of A, what is the apparent FC — and what should it be? Where does the difference come from?
Q2Drag the Sample B depth slider to match Sample A. What happens to the apparent FC bar? Now increase it to 3× or 4×. What does this tell you about comparing samples sequenced to different depths without normalisation?
Q3Switch to Multi-gene detection. Which genes (left or right of the x-axis) are worst affected by depth variation? Move the "True gene proportion" slider to shift the whole distribution and observe when genes switch from red (zero) to blue (detected).
Q4Depth is not the only pre-sequencing step that shapes which genes get counted. PCR amplification is required before sequencing — and it is non-linear. A cDNA molecule copied in the first cycle produces exponentially more copies than one that misses it. What does this mean for a gene expressed at very low levels — and why does it matter for how you interpret a zero in the count matrix?
Q1: The apparent FC matches the depth ratio exactly — 2× deeper sequencing produces 2× the raw count and therefore a 2× apparent fold change, even though the true FC is 1.0 (the gene did not change). The difference comes entirely from the sequencing budget: Sample B received twice as many reads, so every gene — changed or not — appears doubled. The raw count is not a measurement of expression; it is a proportion of the total reads times the budget.
Q2: When both depths are equal the apparent FC bar drops to 1.0× and aligns with the true FC — the artefact disappears. At 3× or 4× depth difference, the apparent FC grows linearly. This is exactly what normalisation (CPM, TPM) corrects: it divides each count by the sample's total reads, converting the count into a proportion that is independent of the sequencing budget.
Q3: Lowly expressed genes (left side, low proportion). A highly expressed gene accumulates many reads even at low depth. A gene at 0.0001% of the library may receive 0 reads at 10M depth and 10 reads at 100M depth — appearing to switch on when only the budget changed. Moving the expression slider down makes more genes red in both samples simultaneously.
Q4: A lowly expressed gene produces few cDNA molecules. If those molecules amplify poorly in early PCR cycles they are drowned out by more efficiently amplifying transcripts and can receive zero reads even though the mRNA was present. This is a sampling zero that arises before sequencing even begins — in addition to the depth-budget problem shown here. It is one reason why a zero in the count matrix can never be assumed to mean biologically absent, which is explored directly in Section 2.

Sparsity & Zero-Inflation

Not all zeros mean the same thing. Biological zeros, technical dropout, and sampling zeros look identical in the count matrix but require completely different analytical responses.

Key distinction: A zero in your matrix could mean (a) the gene is genuinely off, (b) the mRNA was present but not captured, or (c) it was captured but not sampled. The matrix cannot tell you which. Platform knowledge and biological reasoning are required.

Q1Switch between Bulk RNA-seq and 10x Chromium. In Bulk, where do the zeros cluster — top or bottom rows? In 10x, zeros appear throughout — even highly expressed genes show dropout. What does this structural difference tell you about the biological vs technical origin of zeros in each platform?
Q2A T-cell marker gene shows 0 counts in all B cells, and 0 counts in 70% of T cells. Which zeros are biological and which are likely technical? Why does this distinction matter for analysis?
Q3Hou et al. (2020) benchmarked 18 imputation methods and found most did not improve downstream analysis. What does this imply about "filling in" technical zeros?
Q4Switch to Proteomics. The heatmap shows two colours of missing values — red (MNAR) and yellow (MAR). What is the key structural difference between the two patterns? Why must they be imputed differently — and what would go wrong applying mean imputation to the red MNAR block?
Q1: In Bulk, zeros cluster at the bottom rows — the lowly expressed genes. Top rows (highly expressed) are consistently blue across all samples. This structure tells you the zeros are biological: these genes are genuinely not expressed. In 10x, zeros are scattered throughout including the top rows, because the 10–15% per-cell capture rate means even highly expressed genes are often missed. The pattern tells you the zeros are technical — not biological absence but measurement failure.
Q2: The B cell zeros are biological. The T cell zeros are likely technical — the gene should be expressed across all T cells (per prior literature), so the 70% zero rate reflects stochastic capture failure at low expression. Calling those T cell zeros "gene off" would mischaracterise the biology of the entire population.
Q3: Imputation is not a safe default. If a zero is biological, imputing a value creates expression that was never there. You must reason about which type of zero you have — informed by platform, gene expression level, and biology — before deciding whether to impute.
Q4: MNAR (red) forms a solid block at the bottom — systematic, present in nearly all samples for those proteins because low-abundance proteins consistently fall below the instrument's detection limit. MAR (yellow) dots appear scattered randomly across all proteins regardless of abundance. They must be imputed differently: MNAR values are not randomly absent — they are absent because they are low, so imputing the mean of detected values would assign an artificially high abundance to proteins that were actually below the detection threshold, inflating their apparent levels. MNAR requires left-censored or abundance-based approaches (drawing from a distribution below the detection limit). MAR values are randomly absent and can safely use mean or kNN imputation.

Compositionality

Because the sequencing total is fixed, you are always measuring proportions — not absolute quantities. When one feature increases its share, all others must mathematically decrease.

The pie chart problem: Sequencing gives you a pie chart, not a bar chart. A gene's count tells you its share of the total — not how many molecules were present. Standard normalisation fixes between-sample depth but does not remove this within-sample constraint.

Q1Set the kill rate to 99% and species A absolute change to 0. What happens to Species A's relative abundance? Is this a biological expansion?
Q2Now increase Species A's absolute change to +5×. How does this interact with the compositional effect? Can you disentangle the two from relative abundance alone?
Q3Two taxa that are biologically unrelated both decrease slightly after treatment. What kind of spurious correlation might appear between them in a naive Pearson analysis — and why?
Q4Move the "Total reads (before)" slider down to 1–2M. Antibiotic treatment dramatically reduces total bacterial biomass, which often means post-treatment DNA yields are much lower — producing far fewer reads. Beyond the compositional effect you saw in Q1, what additional problem does this depth difference between time points create for the analysis?
Q1: Species A's relative abundance skyrockets even with 0 absolute change. This is a pure compositional artefact — it is the last taxon standing, not a biological winner. Concluding "antibiotic boosts Species A" from this data is a Type I compositional error.
Q2: No. Relative abundance conflates genuine biological expansion with compositional rescue. You cannot distinguish the two without absolute quantification (e.g., spike-in controls or total biomass measurement).
Q3: Spurious negative correlation. In a fixed-sum system, any increase in one taxon forces decreases in all others. Two unrelated taxa sharing the "shrinking slice" dynamic will appear negatively correlated — not from ecology, but from arithmetic.
Q4: A dramatic depth difference between time points creates a second, separate problem on top of compositionality: taxa near the detection limit before treatment may be undetectable afterwards simply because fewer reads were generated — not because they were eradicated. A taxon that receives 50 reads at 10M depth receives 0.5 reads on average at 100k depth, making it invisible. This is the low-biomass technical zero problem: you cannot distinguish genuine loss of a taxon from sequencing failure caused by reduced biomass. Even perfect normalisation cannot recover a species that was never sampled. Spike-in controls or total biomass quantification (e.g. qPCR of universal 16S) are required to distinguish biology from measurement failure.

Why Classical Statistics Fail

The answer is not "t-tests always fail on omics data" — it is more nuanced than that. There is a progression of increasingly adequate approaches, each solving specific problems the previous level leaves behind.

Three specific reasons a raw-count t-test fails:
  1. Counts are not comparable across samples — library sizes and compositional effects distort relative abundances (Sections 1 & 3)
  2. Counts are discrete non-negative integers — the normal approximation underlying t-test theory breaks down at values of 0, 1, 2
  3. Variance estimated from n=3 has only 2 degrees of freedom — the per-gene estimate could be half or double the true value by chance alone

Schurch et al. 2016 key finding: With n=3 replicates per condition — the field norm — tools detect only 20–40% of truly differentially expressed genes. The 48-replicate yeast benchmark confirmed that DESeq2 and edgeR (Level 4) substantially outperform a plain log + t-test (Level 2), and that the gap widens as overdispersion increases. The difference is not the distributional model — it is the empirical Bayes moderation step that stabilises variance estimates by borrowing across genes.

Q4Switch to "Empirical Bayes shrinkage". The red dots (raw per-gene dispersion estimates) scatter widely, especially at low expression. The green dots (shrunk) are pulled toward the yellow trend line. With n=3 replicates, why is sharing information across genes so important — and what would happen to a t-statistic for a gene whose raw variance estimate happened to be very low by chance?
Q5DESeq2 and edgeR model raw counts with the negative binomial distribution. limma-voom instead works on log-CPM values but assigns each observation a precision weight based on its predicted variance, then applies the same empirical Bayes moderation. Both approaches share information across genes. For what type of study might you prefer limma-voom over DESeq2 — and what advantage does the shared framework give when your experiment has multiple factors (e.g. treatment × time × sex)?
Q1: The left panel shows a fan shape — fold changes scatter widely at low expression (left side of the x-axis) and narrow to a tight band at high expression (right side). This is the characteristic MA plot fan. Since no true DE exists, every fold change here is noise generated by unreliable variance estimates at low counts. A lowly expressed gene with counts of 0, 1, 2 across three replicates can produce fold changes of ±2 or more purely by chance — the same instability that makes a plain t-test produce false positives. The right panel shows how EB shrinkage collapses this fan: low-expression fold changes are pulled toward zero because the model knows they are unreliable, while high-expression fold changes are left largely unchanged because they are based on stable counts.
Q2: Level 1 produces excess false positives because every non-DE gene in Group B has approximately 1.5× the raw count of its Group A counterpart — not because of biology, but because Group B was sequenced 50% more deeply. The raw-count t-test cannot distinguish "more expressed" from "more reads." Level 2 (log-CPM) normalises each sample's counts to its total library size before testing, converting raw counts to comparable proportions. After normalisation, depth differences disappear and the false positive rate drops back toward the chance expectation. This is exactly the "counts are proportions of a budget" principle from Section 1.
Q3: False positives increase with lower replicates and higher overdispersion. At n=3, the per-gene variance estimate has only 2 degrees of freedom — it is highly unstable. A gene with genuinely average variability might happen to show low variance across its 3 replicates just by chance, giving an inflated t-statistic and a false positive. Higher overdispersion means the true variability between replicates is larger, so the chance of a misleadingly low variance estimate is also larger. Neither Level 1 nor Level 2 has any mechanism to detect and correct for this — they both use each gene's own noisy variance estimate directly, with no borrowing from other genes.
Q4: With only n=3 replicates, a per-gene variance estimate has just 2 degrees of freedom — it could easily be half or double the true value by chance alone. A gene whose variance happened to be underestimated would produce an inflated t-statistic and a false positive. Empirical Bayes moderation pulls each per-gene estimate toward the genome-wide trend, effectively giving the estimate more degrees of freedom by borrowing from hundreds of genes at similar expression levels. This is the single most important feature shared by DESeq2, edgeR, and limma-voom — it is what makes reliable inference possible at small n.
Q5: limma-voom is particularly well suited to large datasets and complex multi-factor experimental designs. Because it works within a general linear model framework, it handles arbitrary combinations of factors, interactions, and contrasts with the same flexibility as classical linear models — just with precision weights and EB moderation applied. DESeq2 and edgeR also support GLMs, but limma's formula interface is often more expressive for complex designs. limma-voom also works well for proteomics and microarray data, providing a unified framework across data types. For very lowly expressed genes (counts of 0–2), DESeq2/edgeR's native count model has a small advantage; for moderate to high counts the approaches are essentially equivalent.
