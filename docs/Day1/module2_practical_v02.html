What a Count Actually Is

A sequencing count is a proportion of a fixed budget — not an absolute measurement. Explore how sequencing depth shapes what you observe, independently of biology.

Core idea: Gene X received 200 counts in Sample B and 100 in Sample A. Does that mean it's more active? Only if both samples had the same total reads. If Sample B had twice the depth, nothing changed biologically.

Sparsity & Zero-Inflation

Not all zeros mean the same thing. Biological zeros, technical dropout, and sampling zeros look identical in the count matrix but require completely different analytical responses.

Key distinction: A zero in your matrix could mean (a) the gene is genuinely off, (b) the mRNA was present but not captured, or (c) it was captured but not sampled. The matrix cannot tell you which. Platform knowledge and biological reasoning are required.

💬 Discussion Questions
Q1Switch between Bulk RNA-seq and 10x Chromium. The zero rates differ dramatically — which zeros are mostly biological in bulk vs mostly technical in 10x?
Q2A T-cell marker gene shows 0 counts in all B cells, and 0 counts in 70% of T cells. Which zeros are biological and which are likely technical? Why does this distinction matter for analysis?
Q3Hou et al. (2020) benchmarked 18 imputation methods and found most did not improve downstream analysis. What does this imply about "filling in" technical zeros?
Q1: Bulk zeros are mostly biological — genes genuinely unexpressed across thousands of averaged cells. 10x zeros are predominantly technical/sampling, because the 10–15% capture rate and shallow per-cell depth guarantees most transcripts are not detected even when present.
Q2: The B cell zeros are biological. The T cell zeros are likely technical — the gene should be expressed across all T cells (per prior literature), so the 70% zero rate reflects stochastic capture failure at low expression. Calling those T cell zeros "gene off" would mischaracterise the biology of the entire population.
Q3: Imputation is not a safe default. If a zero is biological, imputing a value creates expression that was never there. You must reason about which type of zero you have — informed by platform, gene expression level, and biology — before deciding whether to impute.

Compositionality

Because the sequencing total is fixed, you are always measuring proportions — not absolute quantities. When one feature increases its share, all others must mathematically decrease.

The pie chart problem: Sequencing gives you a pie chart, not a bar chart. A gene's count tells you its share of the total — not how many molecules were present. Standard normalisation fixes between-sample depth but does not remove this within-sample constraint.

Q2: No. Relative abundance conflates genuine biological expansion with compositional rescue. You cannot distinguish the two without absolute quantification (e.g., spike-in controls or total biomass measurement).
Q3: Spurious negative correlation. In a fixed-sum system, any increase in one taxon forces decreases in all others. Two unrelated taxa sharing the "shrinking slice" dynamic will appear negatively correlated — not from ecology, but from arithmetic.

Why Classical Statistics Fail on RNA-seq Data

Why do t-tests fail on RNA-seq data — and what do DESeq2 and edgeR do differently? The answer comes down to three properties you can see directly in the data.

Dataset: 1,000 genes × 6 samples (3 control + 3 treated, 100 truly DE genes). Simulated with published RNA-seq parameters — Love et al. (2014) Genome Biology. See generate_demo_data.R to swap in real counts.

Three things to explore:
  • Mode 1 — Count distribution: RNA-seq counts are not bell-shaped. They are right-skewed — most genes have low counts, a few have very high counts. A t-test assumes a normal distribution, which simply doesn't fit.
  • Mode 2 — MA plot: At low expression, fold-change estimates between conditions are dominated by noise, not biology. The "fan shape" is the signature of this problem.
  • Mode 3 — Variability trend: Lowly expressed genes are always more variable between replicates. DESeq2 and edgeR exploit this genome-wide pattern to make fair comparisons — especially critical when you only have 3 replicates.

Focus on the shapes and what they mean biologically — not the statistical details behind them.

What these three plots tell you — put together:

Mode 1 — RNA-seq counts are not normally distributed. Running a t-test on raw counts is wrong by assumption — the data simply doesn't fit the shape the test requires.

Mode 2 — At low expression (left side of the MA plot), even non-DE genes show large apparent fold-changes. This is noise, not biology. Filtering out low-count genes helps, but doesn't fully solve the problem — you need a model that accounts for this.

Mode 3 — DESeq2 and edgeR fit a genome-wide variability trend and use it to borrow information across all genes. A single gene tested with 3 replicates is unreliable on its own; combined with 20,000 other genes, its variability estimate becomes much more stable. This shared information is the core innovation that makes these tools work.
Schurch et al. 2016: With n=3 replicates per condition — the field norm — tools detect only 20–40% of truly DE genes. The 48-replicate yeast benchmark confirmed that the negative binomial model and information-sharing across genes (DESeq2, edgeR) are essential for reliable results at small n.
💬 Discussion Questions
Q1In Mode 1, why does the log2(count+1) transformation make the distribution look more symmetric — but it still doesn't look perfectly normal? What problem does this leave for a t-test?
Q2In Mode 2, drag the minimum count filter from 0 to 20. What happens to the fan shape? Why is filtering alone not a complete solution to the problem?
Q3In Mode 3, why do lowly expressed genes always show higher variability? What would happen to DESeq2's results if it treated a gene with 3 counts the same as a gene with 3,000 counts?
Q1: Log transformation compresses the right tail — the big numbers get pulled toward the smaller ones, making the shape more symmetric. But zeros and very low counts pile up on the left (you can't log-transform a zero without adding a constant), and discrete counts of 0, 1, 2 never look truly continuous. A t-test on log-counts is better than on raw counts, but the normal approximation still fails for lowly expressed genes.
Q2: The fan collapses — the scatter of non-DE genes around zero shrinks considerably. But filtering is a blunt instrument: you discard potentially real biology at low expression, and the genes that pass still have variable reliability depending on their exact count level. DESeq2/edgeR handle this automatically by weighting genes according to their reliability — no arbitrary threshold needed.
Q3: Low-count genes have high variability because small random fluctuations (one extra read, one missed read) represent a large percentage change when totals are small. A gene with counts 1, 2, 0 in three replicates has an enormous relative spread; a gene with 1000, 980, 1020 does not. If DESeq2 treated all genes equally, lowly expressed genes would swamp the analysis with false positives. Instead, the variability trend tells DESeq2 how much to trust each gene's count data — and that's what makes the test fair.
