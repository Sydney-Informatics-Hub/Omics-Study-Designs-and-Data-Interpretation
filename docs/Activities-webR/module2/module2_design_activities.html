How Reliable Is Chance Balance?

Blocking guarantees the balance that randomisation only makes likely. This activity puts a number on "likely" at the sample sizes omics studies actually run.

The setup: equal numbers of cases and controls, split at random into two processing batches of equal size. A blocked design puts half of each group in each batch, every time. Randomisation gets you the same thing only on average.

Samples per group10

Press Run repeatedly. Each press is a fresh randomisation, exactly as if you had shuffled the samples again before processing. Keep a tally of what you get. The Many allocations view draws 100 at once.

Press Run to draw an allocation
💬 Discussion Questions
Q1Keep One allocation selected with 10 samples per group. Press Run fifteen times and record each split. How many came out exactly balanced (5 cases and 5 controls in each batch)? What was the worst split you saw?
Q2Switch to Many allocations. What percentage of random allocations land exactly balanced? What percentage land at 7:3 or worse? Were your fifteen draws typical?
Q3Stay on Many allocations and move the samples-per-group slider: run at 5, then at 25, then at 50. What happens to the spread of the distribution, and to the percentage landing at 70:30 or worse? At which of these sample sizes would you be comfortable relying on chance alone?
Q4The blocked panel is identical on every press. It costs no extra samples and no extra sequencing. So why is randomisation still necessary, what does it protect you from that blocking cannot?
Q1: With 10 per group, roughly a third of allocations come out exactly 5:5. Most groups will see five or six exact splits out of fifteen, several 6:4 splits, and typically one or two at 7:3. An 8:2 split appears in about one draw in fifty, so most groups will not see one, which is itself the point. Randomisation rarely produces disaster; it routinely produces imbalance.
Q2: About 34% land exactly balanced, meaning two-thirds do not. Roughly 18% land at 7:3 or worse. This is the honest version of the claim: chance balance is not dependable at these sample sizes. Note that "not balanced" is not the same as "confounded"; a 7:3 split is still analysable, but the batch effect is estimated from fewer within-batch contrasts, and precision suffers.
Q3: The distribution tightens sharply around 50:50. At n = 5 severe imbalance is common; by n = 50 it is rare. Watch the exactly-balanced percentage too, because it falls as n grows and that surprises people: with more samples there are more possible splits, so any single one becomes less probable. That is not the number that matters. What threatens a study is not missing perfect balance but landing far enough from it that the technical factor is poorly separated from biology, and that risk drops steadily with n. This is why "randomisation will sort it out" is advice imported from trials running at sample sizes omics studies rarely reach.
Q4: Blocking only works on factors you named in advance. You can block on batch, plate, site and sex because you know they exist. You cannot block on a thermocycler gradient nobody has measured, a reagent lot that changed mid-study, or an operator having a bad afternoon. Randomisation is what stops those unnamed factors from lining up with your comparison. The two tools are not alternatives: block on what you can name, randomise everything else.

Multi-gene Detection: What Depth Makes Visible

The same 25 genes, at the same true abundances, sequenced at two different depths. Nothing biological differs between the panels. Only the budget does.

Core idea: a gene that receives zero reads is not absent; it was not sampled. At shallow depth, low-abundance genes drop out; at greater depth the same genes reappear. Depth sets a practical floor on what can be detected reliably.
⚙ Sequencing Budget
Sample A depth5M
Sample B depth40M
Highest gene abundance0.0010%
The design question. The floor is not chosen arbitrarily, it is set by the least-abundant feature your question depends on. Detecting that a feature is present takes less depth than detecting a change in it.

Press Run to generate plot
💬 Discussion Questions
Q1Run with the defaults (Sample A at 5M, Sample B at 40M). Compare the zero counts in the two subtitles. Which genes ; left or right of the x-axis ; are the ones that vanish, and why those?
Q2Drag Sample A up to match Sample B. What happens to its zeros? Did any gene become more abundant in doing so? What exactly changed between the two runs?
Q3Return Sample A to 5M. Now move the abundance slider down. Watch genes switch from blue to red in both panels at once. If these two samples were a case and a control in your study, what would you conclude about the red genes ; and would you be right?
Q4Suppose you add ten more biological replicates, all sequenced at 5M. Does that recover the genes sitting at zero? Which design lever does, and what does this tell you about the difference between a detection problem and a power problem?
Q1: The genes on the left; the lowest-abundance ones. A highly abundant gene accumulates reads even at shallow depth, so it clears zero comfortably. A gene at a very low proportion may receive no reads at 5M and several at 40M. The abundances are identical across both panels by construction; only the number of reads drawn differs.
Q2: Sample A's zeros drop to match Sample B's. No gene became more abundant ; the true proportions are fixed in the simulation and identical in both panels. All that changed is how many reads were drawn from each library. This is the point the shallow-versus-deep figure in the module makes: the biology did not change between the panels, the budget did.
Q3: You would conclude those genes are absent in both, and you would be wrong ; they are present at the same abundance as always, simply below what 5M reads can reliably sample. The danger in a real study is the asymmetric version: if one group were consistently sequenced shallower than the other, its zeros would look like biological absence. That is depth acting as a confounder, which is a design problem rather than a sample-size one.
Q4: No. More replicates at the same shallow depth give you more samples in which the gene is undetected ; a more confident measurement of nothing. Only depth (or enrichment, or a targeted assay) makes an invisible feature visible. Replication addresses variability between biological units; depth addresses whether the feature is observable at all. They are not interchangeable, which is why the practical rule is to reach adequate depth first and then spend what remains on replication.
